ARCHIVE / 001 — THE CONSTANT

137

A scientific archive, a history of ideas, and a tribute to one of the fundamental constants of nature.

The fine-structure constant, written α, sets the scale of the electromagnetic interaction. It has no units. Its measured value is known with extraordinary precision. Its place in a deeper explanation remains open.

α = 7.2973525643(11) × 10−3

2022 CODATA recommended value. In parentheses, the digits give the standard uncertainty in the final quoted digits: here (11) means 0.0000000011 in the displayed coefficient, before multiplication by 10−3. NIST/CODATA 2022 table.

  • α   7.2973525643(11) × 10−3
  • α−1   137.035999177(21)
  • Approximation   α ≈ 1/137

The familiar 137 is a useful approximation to the reciprocal. It is not the exact reciprocal of the modern measured value.

α = e2 / (4πε0ℏc)

This is the SI expression for α. Together, the constants form a dimensionless ratio: the units cancel. A dimensionless value can be compared across unit systems without changing its number.

e
Elementary electric charge.
ε0
Vacuum permittivity.
ℏ
Reduced Planck constant, h divided by 2π.
c
Speed of light in vacuum.

In quantum electrodynamics, α is the low-energy electromagnetic coupling: a measure of interaction strength that organizes calculations. It is not the literal probability that an electron absorbs a photon. Amplitudes and probabilities depend on the particular quantum process.

Reference: NIST, “Current advances: The fine-structure constant”.

ARCHIVE / 002 — THE SPECTRUM

What does fine structure mean?

Begin with hydrogen: one electron, one proton, and a spectrum made of distinct lines.

Lines, then splittings

Atoms do not emit every possible colour continuously. Their spectra contain lines corresponding to transitions between discrete energy levels. With sufficiently precise observation, some levels resolve into very close neighbours. Those small separations are called fine structure.

A correction with a scale

In 1916 Arnold Sommerfeld extended the Bohr model with elliptical orbits and relativistic corrections. The fine-structure constant appeared naturally and set the scale of those corrections. The old quantum model was an important step, not a complete account: it did not adequately explain electron spin.

In 1928, Paul Dirac's relativistic quantum theory of the electron brought spin into the description and accounted for hydrogen's fine-structure pattern more completely. Modern quantum theory adds further effects, including radiative corrections such as the Lamb shift.

Eh = α2mec2

The Hartree energy Eh is the characteristic nonrelativistic atomic energy scale. Leading fine-structure corrections are smaller by roughly α2 relative to that scale: atomic binding is of order α2mec2, while the correction is of order α4mec2.

A little more about the old model

Sommerfeld's quantized relativistic orbits reproduced important regularities in hydrogen-like spectra. It predated modern wave mechanics and the electron-spin framework. The Dirac equation later supplied a relativistic quantum description in which spin is part of the theory itself; QED then refines energy levels through interactions with the quantized electromagnetic field.

Sources: NIST on Sommerfeld and α; Sommerfeld, 1916; Dirac, 1928.

A CHRONOLOGY OF IDEAS

The number in time

The constant did not arrive in one moment. Related quantities, measurements, models, and explanations accumulated across generations.

  1. BEFORE 1916

    The ingredients

    Helge Kragh's historical study traces discussion of relationships among electric charge, Planck's constant, and the speed of light before Sommerfeld. It gives particular attention to a 1914 paper by G. N. Lewis and E. Q. Adams, whose result was effectively a numerical expression for the later fine-structure constant.

    Kragh, European Journal of Physics (2003)
  2. 1916

    Arnold Sommerfeld

    Sommerfeld's analysis of atomic spectral lines made the small separations quantitatively tractable within the old quantum theory. The dimensionless combination of charge, Planck's constant, and light speed governed the size of relativistic effects. His paper is the famous early home of the constant, not its only prehistory.

    “Zur Quantentheorie der Spektrallinien” · Annalen der Physik
  3. 1928

    Paul Dirac

    Dirac's relativistic electron theory joined quantum mechanics and special relativity, gave electron spin a natural place, and explained the hydrogen fine-structure pattern within a deeper framework.

    “The Quantum Theory of the Electron” · Proceedings of the Royal Society A
  4. 1929

    Arthur Eddington

    Eddington proposed a mathematical argument intended to derive a value associated with 137 from a theory of electric charge. It was a bold historical attempt, not a result accepted as established physics. His main paper was “The Charge of an Electron” in Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 122, 358–369; a short Nature item by Eddington followed later that year.

    Original paper · Proc. R. Soc. A · Eddington, Nature 124, 840 (1929)
  5. 1930s

    Debate and resistance

    Eddington's numerical ideas were debated against measurements and competing interpretations. His 1934 correspondence argued for a factor 137/136 in quantum theory. The exchange belongs to the history of attempts to explain α; it did not establish a modern derivation.

    Eddington, “The Factor 137/136 in Quantum Theory,” Nature 133, 907 (1934)
  6. 1940s–1950s

    Wolfgang Pauli

    Pauli returned to the value of α as a question about the numerical architecture of physical law. He did not discover the constant. He inherited a problem already prominent in atomic physics and kept its unexplained value in view.

    Charles P. Enz, No Time to be Brief (2002)
  7. 1940s–1960s

    Renormalised quantum electrodynamics

    Tomonaga, Schwinger, and Feynman developed methods that made precise, testable QED calculations possible. The coupling matters because predictions are organized in powers of α, while a measured quantity still belongs to a specific process and energy regime. Feynman's lectures helped carry QED's strange precision into public view; no unsupported quotation is needed to make the point.

    Nobel Prize in Physics 1965
  8. 1958 · ZÜRICH

    Room 137

    Pauli died at Zürich's Red Cross Hospital on 15 December 1958. Later biographical accounts recount that he noticed his room number was 137 and took it as an unsettling coincidence. The room-number story is a recollection, not a scientific observation about the cause or meaning of his death.

    CERN Archives biography · Enz's scientific biography
  9. TODAY

    Precision physics

    Modern experiments and QED theory constrain the low-energy value with extraordinary precision. The effective electromagnetic coupling runs with energy: the α appropriate near the Thomson limit is not the same numerical value used at high momentum transfer. The Standard Model uses this measured parameter extremely well, while the question of why its low-energy value is what it is remains open.

    Particle Data Group, “The electromagnetic coupling” (2024)

WHY THIS VALUE?

ARCHIVE / 003 — THE PHYSICIST

The Pauli archive

Wolfgang Pauli was a brilliant, exacting theoretical physicist. His work changed the structure of quantum mechanics. His interest in α belonged to a scientist's persistent question: what fixes the numbers that nature gives us?

In 1925 he formulated the exclusion principle, a rule that transformed the understanding of atomic structure. The Nobel Prize in Physics followed in 1945. As a young physicist in Munich he studied with Arnold Sommerfeld, whose work had made the fine-structure constant famous. Pauli's scientific correspondence and manuscripts, preserved in the CERN Pauli Archive, record physics being made through argument, calculation, and exchange.

Pauli's relationship with Carl Gustav Jung also needs its history kept in proportion. Pauli sought psychological help during a personal crisis in the early 1930s; later, the two exchanged letters and collaborated on questions linking psychology and scientific ideas. That intellectual relationship is part of Pauli's biography. It is not evidence that psychological symbolism explains a measured physical constant.

Sources: Nobel Prize biography; CERN Pauli Archive; ETH Library on Pauli and Jung.

A REPORTED RECOLLECTION · ZÜRICH · DECEMBER 1958

The room

In a commonly repeated account of Pauli's final days, he noticed that his room at the Red Cross Hospital was numbered 137. The story says that the coincidence disturbed him and that he believed he would not leave the hospital alive. Pauli died there on 15 December 1958. The date and hospital are documented by the CERN Archives; the room-number episode is presented here as a later biographical recollection, not as a verified transcript or a scientific fact.

For the biography and recollection trail: Charles P. Enz, No Time to be Brief: A Scientific Biography of Wolfgang Pauli (Oxford University Press, 2002); CERN Archives biography.

Pauli's fascination with α reflects an enduring human desire to understand why the laws of nature have the numerical structure they do. We can tell that story without confusing a life, a coincidence, and a physical theory.

ARCHIVE / 005 — THE UNKNOWN

The question of why

Measurement and explanation are different kinds of knowledge.

Measure

Experiments and theory constrain the low-energy value with great precision. The result is empirical knowledge with an uncertainty that can be stated and improved.

Use

Once measured, α can be used in QED to predict observables. Agreement between prediction and experiment tests the theory and the measurement together.

Derive

A deeper theory would explain why the parameter has this value, rather than simply taking it as an input. The Standard Model has no universally accepted first-principles derivation of the observed low-energy α.

Approximate

A mathematical pattern that resembles 1/137 is not a physical explanation by itself. A proposed relationship earns that status only through a clear theory, independent consequences, and evidence.

Historical derivations such as Eddington's are worth studying as ideas in context. Numerological matches, unsupported geometric formulas, and coincidences are not established derivations.

IF WE KNOW THE VALUE, DO WE UNDERSTAND WHY IT HAS THAT VALUE?

All three statements can be true at once: precise empirical knowledge, successful predictive use, and an unfinished fundamental explanation.

01 · THE SI EXPRESSION

α = e2 / (4πε0ℏc)

The elementary charge and universal constants combine into the low-energy electromagnetic coupling. Since the units cancel, α is dimensionless.

e is elementary charge · ε0 is vacuum permittivity · ℏ is reduced Planck's constant · c is the speed of light in vacuum.

Mathematical note

In rationalized natural units commonly used in particle physics, ℏ = c = ε0 = 1, and the same coupling is written α = e2/(4π). The numerical value of α stays dimensionless; the symbols change with the unit convention.

02 · THE RECIPROCAL

α−1 ≈ 137.036

The inverse is often quoted because it is a compact number near 137. It is the reciprocal of α, not the value of α itself. The historical shorthand α ≈ 1/137 rounds this further.

Mathematical note

The 2022 recommended inverse is 137.035999177(21). Its reciprocal is approximately 0.0072973525643. Rounding the inverse to 137 discards information and must not be mistaken for an exact identity.

03 · THE ATOMIC ENERGY

Eh = α2mec2

The Hartree energy is the characteristic nonrelativistic energy scale of atomic physics, expressed using the electron mass, light speed, and α.

me is electron mass · Eh is one Hartree, about 27.2 electronvolts.

Mathematical note

Hydrogen's leading binding-energy scale is α2mec2; the leading fine-structure terms are smaller by another factor of α2. Thus α2 describes the approximate relative scale of those corrections.
