GAMMA-RAY
BURSTS

THE UNIVERSE IN A FLASH

RELATIVISTIC JETS / ARTISTIC REPRESENTATION
01 / THE EVENT

Some of the most energetic events in the universe happen in seconds.

Somewhere in the distant universe, a star reaches the end of its life. Or two incredibly dense stellar remnants spiral towards one another.

Under extraordinary conditions, a central engine can launch narrow, powerful jets of matter moving extremely close to the speed of light.

When these jets point towards Earth, we may detect an intense flash of gamma rays.

We call these events Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs).

02 / CLASSIFICATION

Two broad classes. Different cosmic origins.

TYPE 01 / LONG GRB

Long-duration bursts

Commonly associated with the collapse of massive, rapidly rotating stars and the formation of a compact central object, often a black hole.

TYPE 02 / SHORT GRB

Short-duration bursts

Commonly associated with the merger of compact objects, particularly two neutron stars, or a neutron star and a black hole.

The traditional classification uses the T90 duration of the prompt gamma-ray emission. Short bursts typically last less than 2 seconds, while long bursts typically last more than 2 seconds. This is a useful classification, not an absolute rule for identifying the progenitor.
03 / ANATOMY OF A BURST

What happens during a gamma-ray burst?

01 / CENTRAL ENGINE

The source

A newly formed compact object and its surrounding environment power the relativistic outflow.

02 / RELATIVISTIC JETS

The acceleration

Narrow jets of plasma are launched at velocities approaching the speed of light.

03 / PROMPT EMISSION

The flash

The relativistic outflow produces intense high-energy radiation, including gamma rays.

04 / AFTERGLOW

The aftermath

As the outflow interacts with surrounding material, radiation can continue across X-ray, optical and radio wavelengths.

04 / OBSERVATION

How do we observe something so distant?

Earth's atmosphere absorbs most incoming gamma rays. This makes space-based observatories essential for detecting cosmic gamma-ray bursts.

Satellites detect the initial flash. Other observatories then rapidly search for the afterglow.

When gravitational waves accompany a compact-object merger, astronomers can combine gravitational-wave and electromagnetic observations to study the same cosmic event.

05 / THE COSMIC CONNECTION

A flash of light. A story of matter.

Gamma-ray bursts allow us to investigate some of the most extreme environments in astrophysics.

Neutron-star mergers, in particular, can produce heavy elements through rapid neutron capture, known as the r-process.

Their electromagnetic counterparts, called kilonovae, help astronomers investigate how elements heavier than iron are produced and distributed throughout the universe.

A brief cosmic event can therefore reveal information about stellar evolution, nuclear physics, relativistic outflows and the chemical evolution of galaxies.
