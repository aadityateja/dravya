GEOCHRONOLOGY

How do we reconstruct a history that began billions of years before us?

Geochronology is the science of determining the age of rocks, minerals, fossils and geological events — and of placing them into the enormous history of our planet.

↓ Enter deep time
01 / THE PLANET

We are here.

Earth formed approximately 4.54 billion years ago. Everything humans have ever seen occupies only an extraordinarily small interval near the very end of that history.

To understand our place in time, geologists divide Earth's history into nested units: Eons → Eras → Periods → Epochs → Ages .

4.54
billion years of Earth history
02 / DEEP TIME

Before complex life.

Most of Earth's history occurred before the appearance of abundant complex animal life. This immense interval is commonly grouped as the Precambrian, comprising the Hadean, Archean and Proterozoic eons.

Hadean

~4.6 – 4.0 billion years ago

Earth formed from material in the early Solar System. The young planet was intensely hot, heavily bombarded by impactors and underwent differentiation into a metallic core, rocky mantle and crust.

As the planet cooled, liquid water and early oceans became possible.

Solar System forms Earth forms Planetary differentiation Early oceans

Archean

~4.0 – 2.5 billion years ago

Earth's crust, oceans and atmosphere evolved. Life existed primarily as microscopic organisms. Some of the oldest evidence for life comes from rocks and structures associated with this immense interval.

The Archean is divided into the Eoarchean, Paleoarchean, Mesoarchean and Neoarchean.

Early microbial life Ancient oceans Crust develops Stromatolites

Proterozoic

2.5 billion – 538.8 million years ago

The Proterozoic witnessed profound changes in Earth's atmosphere and biosphere. Oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event, while eukaryotic cells and increasingly complex organisms appeared.

The Proterozoic contains three eras: Paleoproterozoic, Mesoproterozoic, Neoproterozoic.

Great Oxidation Eukaryotes Supercontinents Snowball Earth episodes Multicellular life

Ediacaran

635 – 538.8 million years ago

The final period of the Proterozoic. Large multicellular organisms populated the oceans, including the enigmatic Ediacaran biota. The biological world was approaching the transition into the Phanerozoic.

Ediacaran organisms Complex multicellular life Precambrian ends
03 / PHANEROZOIC

Life becomes visible.

The Phanerozoic Eon is the part of Earth's history in which abundant fossil evidence records complex life. It is divided into three great eras: Paleozoic · Mesozoic · Cenozoic .

Paleozoic

~538.8 – 251.9 Ma

The age of early complex marine ecosystems, the rise of fishes, colonisation of land by plants and animals, vast forests, amphibians and early reptiles.

Ends with the largest mass extinction known in Earth's history.

Mesozoic

251.9 – 66 Ma

The Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. Dinosaurs diversified, mammals appeared, flowering plants evolved and the supercontinent Pangaea progressively broke apart.

Cenozoic

66 Ma – present

Mammals and birds diversified after the end-Cretaceous extinction. Grasslands expanded, climate changed, primates and hominins evolved, and eventually Homo sapiens appeared.

04 / PALEOZOIC

The ancient seas.

The Paleozoic transformed Earth from a world dominated by marine ecosystems into one containing extensive terrestrial forests and complex land animals.

Cambrian
~538.8 – 486.9 Ma
Rapid diversification of animal body plans; trilobites and other marine organisms become abundant.
Ordovician
~486.9 – 443.1 Ma
Marine biodiversity expands dramatically. Graptolites, brachiopods and marine communities flourish. Ends with a major mass extinction.
Silurian
~443.1 – 419.6 Ma
Jawed fishes diversify. Vascular plants expand onto land and the earliest terrestrial ecosystems develop.
Devonian
~419.6 – 358.9 Ma
Often called the Age of Fishes. Forests spread, seed plants develop and vertebrates begin the transition toward land.
Carboniferous
~358.9 – 298.9 Ma
Vast swamp forests, giant arthropods and abundant coal-forming vegetation. Divided into Mississippian and Pennsylvanian.
Permian
~298.9 – 251.9 Ma
Pangaea dominates the planet. Reptiles and synapsids diversify. Ends with the catastrophic Permian–Triassic mass extinction.
05 / MESOZOIC

The age of dinosaurs.

The Mesozoic lasted almost 186 million years. Pangaea fragmented, dinosaurs became dominant on land, marine reptiles inhabited the oceans and the first birds and mammals evolved.

Triassic
251.9 – 201.4 Ma
Ecosystems recover from the end-Permian extinction. Early dinosaurs and mammals appear. Pangaea begins to fragment.
Jurassic
201.4 – 145 Ma
Dinosaurs diversify dramatically. Giant sauropods dominate many terrestrial ecosystems. Birds evolve from theropod dinosaurs.
Cretaceous
145 – 66 Ma
Flowering plants diversify. Continents move toward their modern arrangement. The period ends with the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction.
06 / CENOZOIC

The world we recognise.

After 66 million years ago, mammals and birds diversified into many ecological niches. The Cenozoic contains the Paleogene, Neogene and Quaternary periods.

Paleocene
66 – 56 Ma
Mammals diversify after the end-Cretaceous extinction.
Eocene
56 – 33.9 Ma
Warm global climates, extensive forests and major diversification of mammals.
Oligocene
33.9 – 23.0 Ma
Global cooling and expansion of grasslands. Major evolutionary changes among mammals.
Miocene
23.0 – 5.33 Ma
Grasslands expand. Apes diversify and major changes occur in terrestrial ecosystems.
Pliocene
5.33 – 2.58 Ma
Cooling continues. Early hominins evolve in Africa.
Pleistocene
2.58 Ma – 11.7 ka
Repeated glacial and interglacial cycles. Homo species spread across the planet.
Holocene
11.7 ka – present
The present interglacial. Agriculture, cities, civilizations, industrialisation and modern society emerge.

The Holocene has its own clock.

The International Commission on Stratigraphy formally subdivides the Holocene into three ages: Greenlandian, Northgrippian, and Meghalayan.

The Meghalayan begins at approximately 4,200 years ago. Its Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point is defined by a speleothem from Mawmluh Cave in Meghalaya, India.

A piece of rock from a cave in India therefore participates in the formal international language used to describe Earth's time.

08 / THE CLOCK

How do we know?

Geological time is reconstructed from physical evidence preserved in rocks, minerals, fossils, sediments, ice cores and geological structures.

RADIOACTIVE DECAY

N(t) = N₀ e−λt

Unstable radioactive isotopes transform into daughter products at predictable rates. By measuring the ratio of parent and daughter isotopes, scientists can determine the time elapsed since a mineral or geological system closed to isotopic exchange.

This is the foundation of radiometric dating.

09 / READING TIME

Earth's clocks.

Different geological materials preserve different clocks. Scientists select methods appropriate to the age and material being investigated.

U–Pb dating

Uranium-lead systems, especially in zircon, provide some of the most powerful tools for dating ancient rocks and geological events.

K–Ar dating

Potassium-40 decays to argon-40 and can be used to date suitable volcanic and geological materials.

Ar–Ar dating

A refined potassium-argon technique widely used in geochronology and volcanic studies.

Radiocarbon

Carbon-14 dating is particularly useful for relatively young organic material, extending back tens of thousands of years.

U–Th dating

Uranium-thorium systems are valuable for dating carbonates such as cave deposits and speleothems.

Stratigraphy

Rock layers, fossils and their relationships establish relative sequences of geological events.

10 / NOW

You are living
in deep time.

A human lifetime is measured in decades. Civilization occupies thousands of years. Homo sapiens occupy a few hundred thousand years. Earth has existed for approximately 4.54 billion years.

Geochronology is our attempt to read that immense memory.
