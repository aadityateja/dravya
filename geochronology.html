01 / WHERE WE ARE

We are here.

We live on planet Earth. Earth rotates around its axis, giving us the cycle of day and night. At the same time, Earth travels around the Sun.

One complete revolution of Earth around the Sun defines approximately one year. We use years to describe the passage of time in our lives.

02 / YOUR CLOCK

How old are you?

When someone asks your age, you answer with a number. That number represents the amount of elapsed time since you were born, usually expressed in years.

Every birthday is a reminder that another year of your life has passed. But now let's ask a much bigger question.

03 / DEEP TIME

How old is Earth?

Earth does not have a birth certificate. Nobody was here 4.5 billion years ago to write down the date. So how can we possibly know?

4.54
billion years

We cannot simply count Earth's birthdays. Instead, we read the evidence preserved in rocks, minerals and geological processes. This is where geochronology begins.

04 / GEOLOGICAL TIME

Deep time needs a language.

Earth's history stretches across billions of years. To make sense of such an enormous span, geologists divide geological time into hierarchical intervals.

05 / THE ROCK RECORD

Rocks remember.

A rock is not merely a piece of Earth. Minerals inside it can preserve evidence about when they formed and what happened afterwards. Geological structures can therefore act as records of Earth's history.

Some minerals contain radioactive isotopes. These isotopes behave like tiny clocks.

06 / THE ATOMIC CLOCK

A clock inside the atom.

Some atomic nuclei are unstable. They transform through radioactive decay. A parent isotope can decay into a daughter isotope at a characteristic statistical rate.

Parent → Daughter

The decay of a radioactive parent follows an exponential law:

N(t) = N₀ e−λt

The half-life is the time required for half of the parent population to decay:

t1/2 = ln(2) / λ
07 / READING THE CLOCK

From atoms to age.

If we know the decay constant of a radioactive isotope and measure the parent and daughter isotopes preserved in a mineral, we can use that information to estimate elapsed time.


This is the fundamental idea behind radiometric dating.

08 / NOT JUST ONE CLOCK

Earth has many clocks.

Different isotopic systems operate over different timescales and suit different geological materials. Geochronologists therefore choose methods according to the question being asked.

U–Pb ancient rocks
K–Ar volcanic history
Ar–Ar geological events
¹⁴C recent past
U–Th younger materials
